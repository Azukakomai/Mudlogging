\section{Gas While Drilling (GWD) and Mud Logging Fundamentals}

In petroleum drilling engineering, mud logging represents the continuous surface extraction, monitoring, and geochemical analysis of rock cuttings and formation fluids carried out of the borehole by circulating drilling mud \citep{ref9, ref12}. As the rotary drill bit grinds through subsurface geological strata, hydrocarbons trapped within the rock pore spaces are crushed and liberated into the circulating drilling fluid.

At the surface, a mechanical gas trap agitator installed at the shaker "possum belly" extracts the volatile gas mixture from the mud stream. A continuous pneumatic sample line transports the gas to a field logging unit equipped with a high-speed gas chromatograph (GC) and a total hydrocarbon flame ionization detector (FID) \citep{ref12, haworth1985}. The chromatographic analysis decomposes the liberated hydrocarbon gas into its individual alkane constituents:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Methane ($C_1$, $\text{CH}_4$):} The lightest, most volatile alkane molecule, characteristic of non-associated dry natural gas reservoirs.
    \item \textbf{Ethane ($C_2$, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$):} Light hydrocarbon gas fraction, associated with gas-condensate and volatile oil caps.
    \item \textbf{Propane ($C_3$, $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$):} Intermediate-weight alkane, serving as a primary discriminator between gaseous and liquid hydrocarbon phases.
    \item \textbf{Iso-butane ($iC_4$) and Normal-butane ($nC_4$, $\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$):} Medium-weight alkanes indicating liquid-associated hydrocarbons and crude oil columns.
    \item \textbf{Iso-pentane ($iC_5$) and Normal-pentane ($nC_5$, $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$):} Heavy hydrocarbon gas fractions, strongly indicative of liquid crude oil accumulations.
    \item \textbf{Total Gas ($TG$):} The aggregate sum of all hydrocarbon gases liberated into the drilling mud, representing the overall magnitude of the formation gas show \citep{ref14}.
\end{itemize}

Because GWD chromatographic data is collected in real time during the initial drilling pass, analyzing the relative proportions, ratios, and compositional trends of these alkane fractions provides the earliest diagnostic insights into reservoir fluid phase, fluid contacts, and formation productivity \citep{haworth1985, ref12}.

\section{Theoretical and Mathematical Formulations of Petrophysical Ratios}

To extract diagnostic intelligence from raw chromatographic gas data, petroleum engineering and petrophysics have established deterministic ratio formulations. The canonical methods documented in petrophysical literature include:

\subsection{Classic and Expanded Pixler Ratios \citep{ref13}}
The Pixler method evaluates light-to-medium hydrocarbon fractions to delineate gas from oil regimes:
\begin{equation}
    R_1 = \frac{C_1}{C_2}, \quad R_2 = \frac{C_1}{C_3}, \quad R_3 = \frac{C_1}{C_4}, \quad R_4 = \frac{C_1}{C_5}
\end{equation}
In accordance with \citet{ref13}, the primary diagnostic cutoff boundaries for $R_1$ and crossplot slopes are defined as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Associated Dry Gas:} $R_1 > 65.0$.
    \item \textbf{Gas / Gas-Condensate Pay:} $15.0 \le R_1 \le 65.0$.
    \item \textbf{Oil Pay Zone (Medium to Light Crude):} $2.0 \le R_1 < 15.0$.
    \item \textbf{Water / Non-Productive Formation:} $R_1 < 2.0$.
    \item \textbf{Pixler Slope Rule ($\Delta R$):} A progressive positive slope ($R_1 < R_2 < R_3 < R_4$) confirms commercial productive pay. A flat, irregular, or negative slope indicates tight, low-permeability rock or water-bearing intervals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expanded Butane Isomer Multipliers}
Tracking methane against separated butane isomers provides sensitivity to heavier, liquid-associated hydrocarbon fractions:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Ratio}_{\text{iC4}} = \frac{C_1}{iC_4}, \quad \text{Ratio}_{\text{nC4}} = \frac{C_1}{nC_4}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gas Pay Threshold:} $\text{Ratio}_{\text{nC4}} \ge 30.0$ (methane dominates over butane isomers).
    \item \textbf{Oil Pay Threshold:} $\text{Ratio}_{\text{nC4}} < 30.0$ (enriched heavy butane indicates liquid crude oil).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Total Gas Volume ($TG$) and Gas Dryness Ratio ($DR$) \citep{ref14}}
When Total Gas is not pre-recorded, it is derived by summing all recorded hydrocarbon gas fractions:
\begin{equation}
    TG = C_1 + C_2 + C_3 + iC_4 + nC_4 + iC_5 + nC_5
\end{equation}
The Dryness Ratio ($DR$) quantifies the methane fraction within the total hydrocarbon gas mixture:
\begin{equation}
    DR = \frac{C_1}{TG}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dry Gas Regime:} $DR \ge 0.85$ (methane concentration $\ge 85\%$).
    \item \textbf{Wet Gas / Light Volatile Oil:} $0.50 \le DR < 0.85$.
    \item \textbf{Heavy Crude Oil / Liquid-Rich Zone:} $DR < 0.50$.
    \item \textbf{Baseline Show Threshold:} $TG \ge 300\text{ ppm}$ or $C_1 \ge 200\text{ ppm}$ (intervals below threshold $\rightarrow$ \textbf{No Show / Barren Rock}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Carbon-Weighted Density Index ($I_{\text{carbon}}$)}
Quantifies carbon density across the gas stream, scaled by carbon atom count:
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{carbon}} = \frac{TG}{C_1 + 2C_2 + 3C_3 + 4iC_4 + 4nC_4 + 5iC_5 + 5nC_5}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Light Hydrocarbon Gas:} $I_{\text{carbon}} \ge 0.80$.
    \item \textbf{Heavy Liquid Column:} $I_{\text{carbon}} < 0.80$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expanded Haworth Show Evaluation Ratios \citep{haworth1985, ref12}}
These three expanded parameters form foundational multi-ratio fluid typing indicators (Wetness $W_h$, Balance $B_h$, and Character $C_h$):
\begin{equation}
    W_h = \left( \frac{C_2 + C_3 + iC_4 + nC_4 + iC_5 + nC_5}{TG} \right) \times 100\%
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    B_h = \frac{C_1 + C_2}{C_3 + iC_4 + nC_4 + iC_5 + nC_5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    C_h = \frac{iC_4 + nC_4 + iC_5 + nC_5}{C_3}
\end{equation}
According to canonical Haworth et al. criteria:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haworth Wetness Ratio ($W_h$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $W_h < 0.5\%$: Very dry gas, non-commercial tight formation, or barren shale.
        \item $0.5\% \le W_h < 17.5\%$: Gas Pay regime ($0.5\% \le W_h < 5.0\%$ indicates dry gas; $5.0\% \le W_h < 17.5\%$ indicates wet gas/condensate).
        \item $17.5\% \le W_h < 40.0\%$: Oil Pay regime (light to medium crude oil).
        \item $W_h \ge 40.0\%$: Heavy, low-gravity residual or dead oil.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haworth Balance Ratio ($B_h$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $B_h > 100.0$: Very dry gas.
        \item $15.0 \le B_h \le 100.0$: Gas pay column.
        \item $0.5 \le B_h < 15.0$: Oil pay column.
        \item $B_h < 0.5$: Heavy residual oil, water, or barren rock.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haworth Character Ratio ($C_h$):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $C_h < 0.5$: Gas phase (light alkane dominance).
        \item $C_h \ge 0.5$: Oil phase (heavier butane/pentane enrichment).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Composite Screening Indicators ($GOW, WBS, GOR$) \citep{whittaker1991, spe2020}}
Continuous composite indicators isolate heavy fluid concentrations and decouple total gas fluctuations:
\begin{equation}
    \text{GOW} = \frac{(C_3 + iC_4 + nC_4 + iC_5 + nC_5) \cdot TG}{TG}, \quad \text{GOW}_{\text{noTG}} = \frac{C_1 \cdot C_2}{C_3 \cdot C_4 \cdot C_5}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    WBS = \frac{\log_{10}(B_h) - 0.903}{2.097} - \frac{\log_{10}(W_h)}{2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{SPE } GOR = \text{Empirical Gas-to-Oil Ratio Proxy}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Whittaker Score ($WBS$):} $WBS > 2.0 \rightarrow$ Gas Pay; $0.0 \le WBS \le 2.0 \rightarrow$ Oil Pay; $WBS < 0.0 \rightarrow$ Water / Non-Productive.
    \item \textbf{Composite $GOW_{\text{noTG}}$:} $GOW > 10.0 \rightarrow$ Gas Pay; $1.0 \le GOW \le 10.0 \rightarrow$ Oil Pay; $GOW < 1.0 \rightarrow$ Water / Residual.
    \item \textbf{SPE GOR Proxy:} $GOR \ge 3000\text{ scf/bbl} \rightarrow$ Gas / Condensate; $GOR < 3000\text{ scf/bbl} \rightarrow$ Oil column.
\end{itemize}

\section{Critical Review of Existing Formation Evaluation Softwares}

The interpretation of well log and mud log data has historically depended on the expertise of petrophysicists supported by specialized computer-aided formation evaluation software \citep{ref8, wood2020}. Over past decades, commercial and programmatic software platforms have transformed wellsite operations by replacing paper strip logs with digital visualization environments \citep{wood2020}. However, an objective examination of current software tools reveals that existing applications operate predominantly as \textit{data plotters and curve visualizers} rather than proactive forecasting systems \citep{ref8, wood2020}.

\subsection{Commercial Enterprise Software Suites}
In enterprise petroleum operations, several dominant software platforms are widely deployed across exploration and production assets:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Schlumberger (SLB) Techlog and AspenTech Geolog:} These platforms represent the high-end enterprise standard for integrated petrophysics, borehole geology, and geomechanics \citep{wood2020}.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Capabilities:} They provide robust graphical rendering engines capable of displaying hundreds of synchronous curve tracks alongside high-resolution borehole images, full support for industry standard LAS/DLIS formats, and deep integration with geological modeling databases.
        \item \textit{Limitations:} They are massive, heavyweight workstation applications burdened by cost-prohibitive licensing fees (frequently exceeding \$20,000 to \$50,000 per annual seat license) \citep{wood2020}. Crucially, regarding Gas While Drilling (GWD) data, they function primarily as passive visualizers; calculating expanded multi-ratio indicators or custom fluid predictions requires writing complex scripts in specialized proprietary consoles (e.g., Techlog Python Script Editor or Geolog Loglan macro language) or developing compiled C++ DLL plugins \citep{wood2020}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{WellCAD (Advanced Logic Technology) and LogPlot (RockWare):} These tools are widely utilized industry standards for composite well log presentation and geotechnical strip log drafting.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Capabilities:} Outstanding layout customization for lithological symbols, mud log visual tracks, and standardized PDF export formatting tailored for wellsite operational reports.
        \item \textit{Limitations:} They are strictly visual drafting and plotting engines with no built-in petrophysical calculation logic. They plot raw chromatographic gas values ($C_1 - C_5$) as static visual lines but possess no automated algorithms to derive ratio matrices or forecast hydrocarbon fluid regimes.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Open-Source and Programmatic Log Plotters}
In the open-source domain, several Python libraries (such as \texttt{lasio}, \texttt{welly}, and standard \texttt{matplotlib/plotly} scripts) have gained popularity for petrophysical data handling \citep{wood2020}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Capabilities:} They offer free, scriptable parsing of standard LAS files and flexible 2D line plotting without commercial licensing barriers.
    \item \textit{Limitations:} They lack an integrated, user-friendly graphical user interface (GUI), requiring users to write manual Python code for every calculation and visual track setup. Furthermore, they contain no automated GWD multi-ratio interpretation pipelines or deterministic fluid-typing decision trees, leaving them as low-level programmatic drafting utilities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Machine Learning Approaches in Formation Evaluation}

Parallel to classical formation evaluation techniques, the application of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) to subsurface prediction has grown substantially since its early introduction to petroleum engineering \citep{ref1}. The conceptual groundwork for applying artificial intelligence techniques to oilfield problems was laid by \citet{ref1}, who demonstrated that Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) could approximate complex, non-linear mappings between well log inputs and reservoir properties that resist explicit analytical formulation. Over the subsequent decades, this research trajectory has expanded across a wide range of formation evaluation and logging tasks:

\subsection{In-Situ Parameter and Reservoir Property Prediction}
Machine learning models have been widely investigated to predict physical and geomechanical parameters from conventional log suites where direct physical measurement is operationally difficult or cost-prohibitive. For instance, \citet{ref5} demonstrated that supervised machine learning algorithms trained on wireline log suites can predict in-situ rock stresses with high statistical accuracy. Similar architectures, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP), have been implemented to predict permeability, shear wave velocity ($V_s$), and total organic carbon (TOC) from standard gamma ray, resistivity, and density-neutron inputs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Capabilities:} These models excel at approximating complex, non-linear relationships between multi-sensor wireline curves and continuous rock mechanical parameters without requiring explicit physical parameterization, providing rapid estimations when direct physical measurements are cost-prohibitive.
    \item \textit{Limitations:} Supervised regressors depend heavily on vast volumes of historical calibration data. Their "black-box" nature obscures internal reasoning, rendering continuous parameter estimations non-auditable during formal regulatory and reserve audits \citep{ref1, ref3}. Furthermore, they are highly sensitive to geological distribution shift, frequently suffering severe extrapolation errors when transferred to different geological formations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Well-Log Reconstruction and Missing Curve Generation}
A frequent operational challenge in legacy wells is the presence of incomplete or degraded log suites. Data-driven algorithms have emerged as powerful tools for curve synthesis and data completion. \citet{ref4} applied ensemble machine learning techniques, specifically gradient-boosted decision trees, for automatic well-log completion and generation across complex geological intervals in the Ordos Basin. Their findings demonstrated that ensemble regressors can effectively reconstruct missing curves by learning statistical correlations across adjacent, intact log tracks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Capabilities:} Ensemble tree algorithms can effectively restore missing or corrupted log tracks by exploiting multivariate statistical correlations across intact adjacent channels, reducing the need for costly well re-logging.
    \item \textit{Limitations:} In frontier exploration and wildcat drilling where offset well data is scarce or nonexistent, these models cannot be reliably trained or deployed \citep{ref1}. Moreover, because ensemble reconstructions rely on empirical correlations rather than explicit petrophysical causality, they risk generating physically unrealistic or hallucinated curve artifacts when encountering atypical lithologies, failing strict SOP verification standards \citep{ref3}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deep Learning and Graph Neural Architectures}
More recently, advanced deep learning architectures have been introduced to handle flexible and unstructured logging configurations. \citet{ref6} developed \textit{FlexLogNet}, a flexible graph-neural-network-based well-log completion method designed to adaptively utilize whatever log channels are available in a given borehole to predict missing curves. Such deep learning architectures capture complex spatial and inter-curve dependencies, providing high predictive performance across multi-well datasets.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Capabilities:} Graph neural networks and deep sequence models handle heterogeneous, missing, or irregular channel configurations dynamically, effectively capturing multi-scale depth dependencies and spatial variations across complex multi-well datasets.
    \item \textit{Limitations:} These deep architectures introduce extreme algorithmic opacity and heavy computational/GPU training overheads, making them impractical for lightweight wellsite edge deployment. Furthermore, their latent representations cannot be audited or justified under standard petrophysical operating procedures, and they remain vulnerable to out-of-distribution geological shifts across differing depositional basins.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Facies and Hydrocarbon Zone Classification vs. Regulatory PRMS Audits}
In the domain of automated classification, supervised classification algorithms (such as Random Forests, XGBoost, and deep convolutional networks) have been trained on historical petrophysical datasets to classify lithofacies boundaries and discriminate pay zones from non-productive intervals. These models learn decision boundaries directly from multi-dimensional log feature vectors, achieving high classification scores within targeted training fields.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Capabilities:} Supervised classifiers can rapidly segment multi-dimensional log datasets and map non-linear decision boundaries between distinct lithologies and productive pay zones in well-characterized, mature fields with abundant labeled data.
    \item \textit{Limitations:} Non-transparent decision boundaries violate SPE PRMS Chapter 4 deterministic auditability mandates, which strictly require physical, documented methodologies for hydrocarbon reserve certification \citep{ref2, ref3}. Additionally, supervised classifiers require extensive, pre-labeled ground-truth training datasets (such as core analyses or production tests) that are unavailable during initial first-drilling GWD operations in wildcat wells.
\end{itemize}
